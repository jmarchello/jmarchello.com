# Why Do You Use Scrum?

I've been thinking about the purpose of Scrum a lot lately. I've been a part of many teams that use it, but it always seems to take up more time than its worth. We spend a ton of time agonizing over how many story points a given issue is worth, then spend even more time deciding if we can fit one more issue in the sprint. After all that time and work, the sprint would start and we'd either blow away our entire's sprint work in a few days or only get to half of our committed work. The promise was that the more we did this, the better we would get at estimating our work and the more we would hit our commitments. After years of doing this, I've seen little to no improvement in the predictability of a sprint. "Predictability" is often one of the main reasons I hear for using Scrum. If that's the case, why does this promise never seem to come to fruition?

It's questions like this that led me to dig deeper into what Scrum is and what it's core motiviations are. If not predictability than what? This led me to the [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html), written and maintained by the original creators of the Scrum framework: Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. Using the Scrum Guide as a reference I want to explore the reasons organizations may adopt scrum and how it is often misunderstood and misused.

## Predictability

Software development is notoriously difficult to estimate. The reasons for this are legion and have been anylized ad nauseum elsewhere. I'll simply argue that to describe a task and then estimate how long that task will take is nearly impossible in software engineering and should not be attempted. What we should do instead is set a timebox and define what value we need at the end of that timebox. Then we work to provide that value, adjusting scope as we go to ensure it can be provided within the timebox. In short, we should shape the work to a timeframe, rather than guessing the timeframe based on the work. This creates _true_ predictability because the stakeholders can confidently predict what value they're getting and when.

I think this was the intention behind Sprints in Scrum. The Scrum guide says the following about Sprints and predictability:

> Sprints enable predictability by ensuring inspection and adaptation of progress toward a Product Goal at least every calendar month.

So predictability is created via a _goal_, rather than estimation. This point is supported further when the Scrum Guide is describing the responsibilies of developers on a Scrum Team:

> If the work turns out to be different than they expected, they collaborate with the Product Owner to negotiate the scope of the Sprint Backlog within the Sprint without affecting the Sprint Goal.

When our understanding or priorities change, we don't adjust the goal or our estimates, we adjust the scope to ensure we still meet out goal. Scrum could be defined as "Goal driven development". And let me state the obvious here, things will _always_ change, even mid-sprint. Scrum is designed for that change. The fact that our understanding of a task evolves _as we execute_ is at the very core of why Scrum and Agile exist. We are _expected_ to learn and adjust as we design and develop a given piece of value.
